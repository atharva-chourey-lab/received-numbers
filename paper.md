# Received numbers: a bearer-by-bearer audit of famous figures in Indian history

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

Famous figures in Indian history circulate detached from the texts that first carried them: a number is repeated until its bearer, its scope, and its evidence class are forgotten. This paper audits a defined set of such figures bearer by bearer, asking who first stated each one, what exactly was counted, and whether an independent record can check it. The instrument is the Chinese-correlation record — Tang and Song annals, translation colophons, and dated pilgrim reports — graded under rules registered before any case was examined. A flagship case study assembles all 49 graded quantities for Nalanda: the celebrated 10,000 is carried by Huili's biography of Xuanzang (c. 665–688), counting residents and visitors together, while Xuanzang's own *Records* say "several thousand" and Yijing reports more than 3,000 residents; the excavated plant (205 plaque-counted cells in 6 of 11 vihāras; an estimated 450–650 cells in total) gives a design capacity of 450–650 residents and an absolute ceiling below 2,000, and the defensible peak is c. 2,500–4,500 residents, centred on 3,000–3,500. Two pre-registered campaigns then grade 37 further cases: Campaign 1 returns 5 corrected, 3 corroborated, 5 bounded and 2 no-bite verdicts; Campaign 2 returns 2, 4, 8 and 8. Every verdict, including every no-bite, is published with the strongest objection to itself.

**Keywords:** Nalanda; Xuanzang; Huili; Yijing; Chinese annals; chronology; source criticism

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Received numbers as a failure mode

A received number is a quantity that has outlived its evidence. It is quoted from textbook to textbook with the citation, if any survives, pointing to another secondary work rather than to the text that first carried the figure. Three failures are usually compounded. *Bearer drift*: the figure is credited to a more authoritative witness than the one who stated it. *Scope drift*: a figure stated for one population — residents and visitors together — is repeated for a narrower one, residents alone. *Class drift*: a round, panegyric magnitude is repeated with the confidence appropriate to a count.

Nalanda's 10,000 exhibits all three (§3): its bearer is not Xuanzang but his disciple Huili; its scope is residents and visitors combined; its class is rhetoric. None of this is new in the primary literature: Beal's translations already carry the contrast (Beal 1884; Beal 1911), and we claim no novelty for the attribution itself. What this audit adds is quantification and set-level discipline: the figure is tested against the excavated plant and its endowment, the master-to-disciple inflation is measured, and the same grading is applied, under pre-registered rules, to 37 further cases. The failure mode is also chronological — a regnal sum in a chronicle composed centuries after its kings, a 400–600-year lifespan in hagiography, a 218-year interval carried by one lineage's arithmetic — and is audited the same way.

### 1.2 The bearer-by-bearer method

For each figure: (1) identify the earliest surviving bearer — the specific text, inscription, or excavation record, with its date; (2) transcribe what that bearer actually counts, including its stated scope; (3) grade its evidence class (dated court record, dated colophon, eyewitness observation, transmitted tradition, modern derivation); (4) test it against any independent instrument bearing on the same quantity — a second witness, an epigraphic rate, or the physical plant; (5) publish a verdict from a fixed class list, with the strongest objection to that verdict.

Tracing a repeated claim through its citation chain to an originating bearer who does not support it is an established method in the study of academic urban legends and citation distortion, demonstrated for scientific and medical claims by Greenberg (2009), Sutton (2010), and Rekdal (2014), whom we credit as the method's precedents; we claim no novelty for the method itself. The narrower contribution is application as a set: to our knowledge this is the first systematic bearer-by-bearer audit of a defined set of received historical figures — enrolments, populations, dates, and floruits in Indian history — graded under one scheme with the full denominator published. This wording is the rescoped form adopted after a precedent sweep (see the accompanying sources map).

### 1.3 The Chinese-correlation instrument

The independent instrument for the chronological cases is the Chinese record of India: dynastic annals recording embassies and investitures on dated days, translation bureaus whose colophons date a translation to a year, catalogues, and the travel records of pilgrims — Faxian, Xuanzang, Yijing, Song Yun, Hyecho — resident in India at dated times. Its edge is sharp and its blunt side known, and the campaigns' central finding is the line between them. The Chinese record is an independent witness only when the event it records happened on Chinese soil or at a Chinese desk: an embassy received, an investiture issued, a translation completed, a pilgrim present. When a Chinese text instead transmits an Indian institution's own numbers — a lineage's interval, a chronicle's sum — it inherits that institution's construction, and its agreement with Indian tradition proves nothing. Reported intervals inside pilgrim and biographical texts are therefore graded as tradition wherever they appear, including when they arrive in Chinese, and including when the Chinese tradition itself manufactured them.

## 2. Methods

**Pre-registration.** Both campaigns were declared before any case was graded. Campaign 1 registered 15 cases, including two calibration cases already established in the underlying spine catalogue (Bhartṛhari; Harṣa) and one deliberate expected-no-bite control (Śaṅkara). Campaign 2 registered scopes A–D in advance — the Buddha chronology war, Kṛṣṇa as its expected-no-bite control, Vikramāditya bearer by bearer, and a sweep of major kings — with scope E (Mahāvīra and the śramaṇa world) added by addendum and registered before any E case was graded. One Campaign 2 case (D8, further Chola material) was excluded under its own pre-registered rule — graded only if material genuinely new relative to Campaign 1 was found; none was — and is reported as SKIPPED, so the denominator stays honest. Campaign 1's Bodhidharma addendum ran as a dedicated build outside the 15-case sweep and is counted in neither tally. Pre-registration matters because the verdict classes include failure: a campaign free to drop its nulls could manufacture a perfect hit rate.

**Verdict classes.** CORRECTED — the independent record changes the accepted answer. CORROBORATED — it independently confirms it. BOUNDED — it narrows one side only (a terminus, an excluded candidate, an interval). NO BITE — it does not reach the question, including apparent corroboration that turns out not to be independent. For Nalanda a parallel grading is used: FIRM (dated, inspectable, or independently corroborated), PROBABLE (credible witness, some rounding or inference), RHETORIC (round, panegyric, late, or unsourced), IMPOSSIBLE-BY-CAPACITY (exceeds the physical plant under any stated occupancy assumption).

**Grading discipline.** The Chinese record governs the event it directly records — no more. A colophon gives a *terminus ante quem* (TAQ) minus an unknown lag; the records give a lag median of approximately 160 years, 80% interval approximately 35–400 years (Campaign 1), restated as approximately 160–186 years on five solid cases (Campaign 2); we report both as given, and treat lag-derived placements as probable, never firm. A pilgrim dates what he saw, nothing upstream: pilgrims are exact about what they see and, as Case 11 shows, careless about elapsed centuries. For Nalanda, analogously: a disciple's biography is hearsay for magnitudes even when its lists are precise; epigraphy outranks literature; a claim exceeding the physical plant is graded impossible, not "approximately right."

**Strongest-objection rule.** Each case in the records carries the strongest objection to its own verdict; we preserve that practice in summary below.

## 3. Case study: Nalanda

### 3.1 The dataset

The Nalanda build assembles every number the sources give into one dataset of 49 graded quantities. Two structural facts precede any figure. Every population number for Nalanda's peak comes from exactly three men in one 50-year window (637–691), two of them master and disciple. And no Indian inscription ever numbers the sangha: epigraphy numbers only things given — villages, endowments, buildings — never people. On population the pilgrims stand alone, which is why the capacity test (§3.3) matters.

The witnesses: Faxian (c. 410) found a village with Śāriputra's stupa and no monastery — the firm zero point. Xuanzang, resident 637–642, compiled his *Records* in 646. Huili's *Life* (completed by Yancong, c. 665–688) is a disciple's panegyric. Yijing, resident 673–685, sent his *Record* and *Memoir* home in 691 in an administrative register. Hyecho (723–727/728) attests presence, no figure. Epigraphy includes Yaśovarman's inscription (c. 725–752), funding daily rice, curds and ghee, and Devapāla's plate, regnal year 39 (c. 849–861), granting 5 villages for one foreign-built vihara. The series ends not in 1193 but in 1235, when Dharmasvāmin counted approximately 70 students under a teacher aged over 90, with 2 serviceable viharas. The *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* (c. 1260), usually cited for a sack, does not name Nalanda and is identified by most modern scholars, in the records' assessment, with Odantapuri.

### 3.2 Who said 10,000 — and what was counted

Xuanzang's *Records* describe the priests as "several thousand" (PROBABLE: eyewitness, uncounted, but the master's own text). The 10,000 is Huili's, explicitly residents *and visitors together* (RHETORIC). Yijing reports more than 3,000 monks in the *Record* — a manuscript variant reads 5,000, which Takakusu, as cited in the records, judges the less correct reading — and 3,500 in the *Memoir* (both PROBABLE).

The three witnesses agree exactly where agreement can be checked: all give 8 halls, and Yijing's "more than 300 apartments" matches the excavated ground-floor estimate (§3.3). They diverge by a factor of approximately 3 only on the crowd — in the records' formulation, agreement on everything countable and divergence only on the crowd is the signature of crowd rhetoric, not measurement error. A round-number census sharpens it: of Huili's headline figures (10,000; mastery tiers 1,000 and 500; 100 pulpits; approximately 100 villages; 200 householders per village), six of six are round; his one precise datum is a guest-ration list (120 jāmbīra fruits, 20 areca nuts, 20 nutmegs, 1 ounce of camphor, 1 peck of Mahāśāli rice daily) — precise because it is a list, not a magnitude. In this dataset precision is inversely proportional to size: small numbers are counted (70 students, 8 halls), large numbers liturgical (10,000; Yijing's own 500,000 ślokas, RHETORIC, while his 400 collected texts stand PROBABLE). Two modern figures dissolve here: "1,500 faculty" is a sum of Huili's 1,000 + 500 attainment tiers, not a staff roster; "2,000 teachers" has no pre-modern source at all.

On novelty, §1.1's hold applies in full: the Huili-not-Xuanzang attribution is already in Beal (1884; 1911) and is not a finding of this audit. The section's contribution is quantification (§§3.3–3.5) and the audit-as-a-set. Documenting how widely textbooks still credit Xuanzang directly would strengthen the point, but the underlying records contain no textbook survey; we flag that documentation as a gap (§6) rather than assert it.

### 3.3 The capacity test

The ASI record (1915–1937, 1974–1982) gives 11 vihāras and 14 temples in a 23-hectare protected area, the excavated strip approximately 12 hectares. Plaques transcribe cell counts for six viharas — Monastery 1 (upper) 34; Monastery 4, 20; Monastery 5, 22; Monastery 6, 64 over two levels; Monastery 9, 34; Monastery 11, 31 — totalling **205 cells in 6 of 11 vihāras**. Monastery 5's rear row of 8 may be stores (residential 14–22); Monastery 1's lower level and Monasteries 7, 8, 10 are uncounted. Assigning uncounted standard-plan units the observed 20–34 range gives an estimated 250–320 ground-floor cells — the convergence with Yijing's 300+. Staircases attest second storeys in seven viharas; a full upper ring throughout gives an estimated **450–650 cells in total**, already discounting partial floors. Cells are approximately 3 m × 3 m with one bed platform and a book niche: designed occupancy is one.

| Assumption | Residential capacity, excavated core |
|---|---|
| 1 per cell (design) | 450–650 |
| 2 per cell | 900–1,300 |
| 3 per cell (dormitory crowding) | 1,350–1,950 — absolute ceiling, still below 2,000 |

Huili's 10,000 as residents is therefore IMPOSSIBLE-BY-CAPACITY, needing five to fifteen times the excavated plant at any occupancy. Yijing's 3,000–3,500 exceeds the excavated core but is carriable, because the excavated strip is not the whole walled site. The records price the rescues: if the full complex held approximately twice the excavated stock (approximately 900–1,300 cells), at two per cell that carries 1,800–2,600 — roughly two-thirds to four-fifths of Yijing's figure — with shared occupancy carrying most of the remainder; day scholars and servants inflate the fed population, not the resident sangha, and are credited with hundreds, not thousands. Huili's figure cannot be rescued by the same means stacked. Phase caveat, as recorded: the excavated viharas are mostly late rebuilds on earlier footprints, but on the same plan inside the same enclosure (Monastery 7 shows three successive monasteries on one footprint), so the test measures the site's carrying envelope across its life — the right instrument for peak-century claims.

### 3.4 The endowment cross-check

Carrying capacity is villages × monks per village, and the sources imply three rates. Devapāla's plate (FIRM): 5 villages for one vihara of approximately 30–60 residents, i.e. approximately 6–12 monks per village. Yijing's pair (>3,000 on 200+ cumulative villages) implies approximately 15 — the plate's order. Huili's pair (10,000 on approximately 100 villages) implies 100 per village, an order of magnitude above the epigraphic rate, from his own text's own numbers. Run forward at approximately 10–15 per village: Huili's 100 villages support **1,000–1,500**; Yijing's 200+ support 2,000–3,000+ — landing on Yijing's population and under Huili's. Even at 25 per village, 100 villages feed 2,500, not 10,000: Huili's population is refuted by Huili's endowment. His levy mechanism (200 × 100 = 20,000 households delivering daily) is RHETORIC, scaled to the claim it feeds; both village grants themselves are PROBABLE. No naive villages ratio may be run: Huili's 100 are Harṣa's grant alone, Yijing's 200+ cumulative.

### 3.5 Inflation coefficient and defensible peak

Paired ratios: residents, "several thousand" against >3,000 (Memoir 3,500), approximately 1.0 — master and later pilgrim agree; residents, Huili's 10,000 against >3,000, **3.3** (2.9 against 3,500); halls, 8 against 8, exactly 1.0. The inflation coefficient is approximately 3, located precisely in the step from Xuanzang's *Records* to Huili's *Life*. Assembling only what survived grading — the eyewitness bracket, the capacity envelope (the whole walled complex at the approximately two-per-cell crowding Yijing requires holds roughly 2,000–4,000; materially above approximately 4,500 would need a complex several times the excavated one, for which there is no evidence), and the endowment check — the records state the peak, reproduced exactly:

> **Peak resident population of Nalanda (7th century): c. 2,500–4,500, centred on ~3,000–3,500.**

Total souls at a peak moment plausibly reached approximately 5,000, offered in the records as the possible kernel panegyric rounded to a myriad. The library legend grades weakest: the three Dharmagañja buildings and nine-storey Ratnodadhi arrive via Tāranātha (1608), RHETORIC; "9 million manuscripts" has no pre-modern source; the months-long fire has no dated carrier. What survives is smaller: 657 texts in Xuanzang's tradition (PROBABLE), Yijing's 400 texts (PROBABLE), and copying funded by charter in Devapāla's plate (FIRM).

## 4. Campaign 1: fifteen pre-registered cases

Campaign 1 graded 15 cases: **5 CORRECTED, 3 CORROBORATED, 5 BOUNDED, 2 NO BITE**. The two no-bites are the control working as designed.

### 4.1 Flagship: Kashmir — a generation moved

Kashmir's chronology rests on Kalhaṇa's *Rājataraṅgiṇī* (c. 1148–1150), whose regnal sums place Candrapīḍa at 680–688 and Lalitāditya's 36-year-7-month reign at 699–736 (Stein's computation). The Tang annals' Kashmir entry (*Xin Tang shu*) is a dated diplomatic file: an embassy from Candrapīḍa in **713**; his investiture by edict in **720**; an edict of **722** praising Kashmir's provisioning of Tang troops in Gilgit; and, after him, his brother Muduobi — Stein's identification with Muktāpīḍa (Lalitāditya), judged in the records beyond doubt — requesting investiture in an embassy Stein dates after the Baltistan operations of 736–747. Xuanzang's Kashmir residence (631–633) pegs the dynasty's other end. Candrapīḍa is thus reigning and invested in 719/720, but dead by 688 on Kalhaṇa. Cunningham computed the error at "not less than twenty-five years," and **exactly 31 years** by the 720 investiture; the records summarise the segment shift as approximately **25–36 years**. The modern chronology follows: Durlabhavardhana c. 625–661 (correctly the king enthroned when Xuanzang arrived in 631), Candrapīḍa c. 712/13–720, Lalitāditya c. 724/725–760/761. **CORRECTED** — firm for the annalistic events, probable for the name equations. Strongest objection: the correction is local; the annals touch only the segment they transact with (631–747), cannot repair Kalhaṇa's deep chronology, and assume his relative regnal years are sound and only his epoch mis-set. The records call this the campaign's biggest new correction (Harṣa, below, is the larger single fact but a declared calibration case).

### 4.2 The remaining cases

**Nepal (Case 10, CORRECTED, Probable).** The *Jiu Tang shu* Nepal account, from reports of the 640s, states that Narendradeva's uncle Viṣṇugupta seized the throne, that Narendradeva fled to Tibet, and that the Tibetans re-established him — the restoration pegged to **641** by the Tibetan frame (the standard peg within a 641–643 window, not a year stated in so many words). Nepal supplied 7,000 troops to Wang Xuance's 648 campaign, and the same record complex describes Narendradeva reigning in information current to c. 648: the segment's only absolute peg. Objection: the Tang narrative compresses the inscriptional Jiṣṇugupta/Dhruvadeva tangle into a single usurping uncle.

**The dotted record (Case 1, NO BITE — dependent).** Preserved in Fei Changfang's *Lidai Sanbao Ji* (597) and referred to by Daoxuan (664), the record counts one dot yearly after the parinirvāṇa; Saṅghabhadra placed the **975th dot** at Canton in **489 CE**, yielding 486 BCE (489 − 974) — identical to the corrected long chronology (c. 268 + 218 = 486). The identity is the problem: the custodian chain (Upāli through Moggaliputta Tissa) is, name for name, the Theravāda Vinaya lineage of the *Dīpavaṃsa*, and its last custodian translated that lineage's own Vinaya commentary. One tradition's arithmetic agreeing with itself is one witness, not two; it cannot arbitrate 486 against c. 400 BCE. Objection (Gombrich's defence, as recorded): a ritual count is a practice, harder to retro-fit than king lists. The campaign's answer: the custodians are the same institution that produced the 218-year sum.

**Kaniṣka (Case 2, BOUNDED, Probable).** No Chinese source dates Kaniṣka or his era — a verified negative. The *Sanguozhi* dates the dynasty's far end: under Taihe 3 = **229 CE**, Bodiao, king of the Da Yuezhi — the standard identification of Vāsudeva I, whose inscriptions run era years 64–98 — sent an envoy to Wei. Hence epoch ≤ 165, and with the *Hou Hanshu* horizon (reports to c. 125 naming only Kaniṣka's grandfather's generation) the epoch is confined to **c. 125–165 CE**; **78 CE is excluded** (it would place the 229 embassy in era year 151); 127 vs 144 is undecided. Objection: if Bodiao is a later namesake (a Vāsudeva II), the upper bound dissolves.

**Calibration and rest, in brief.** Bhartṛhari (7, CORRECTED): Yijing (691), "forty years since his death" → death **c. 651–652**; firm as record, probable as history (the two-Bhartṛhari hatch is unclosable from the Chinese side). Harṣa (8, CORRECTED, Firm): embassy 641; Kannauj reception 643; the mission dispatched 647 finds him dead — **death 647**, reign **[606, 647]**, a date no Indian source supplies. Nāgārjuna (4, CORRECTED, firm TAQ side): the *Mūlamadhyamakārikā* translation of **409** closes the 400–600-year lifespans; floruit c. 150–250. Aśvaghoṣa (3, BOUNDED): TAQ **421**; c. 100–200. Asaṅga–Vasubandhu (5, BOUNDED): frame c. 300–550; the *Life*'s intervals are tradition arithmetic. Dignāga–Dharmakīrti (6, BOUNDED): Dignāga TAQ 649; Dharmakīrti bounded as reception — not famous in 629–645, famous by 691 — c. 550–660 compatible once silence is priced as silence. Hūṇas (11, CORROBORATED, Probable): Song Yun's **520** eyewitness fits Mihirakula's floruit (c. 515–542); Xuanzang's "several hundred years" is a logged Chinese-side error (true gap ~100 years). Gupta frame (12, CORROBORATED, Probable): Faxian's years (c. 401–411) fall inside Candragupta II's reign — king unnamed, so the frame's consistency is what is corroborated. Cholas (13, CORROBORATED, Firm): Rājarāja (**1015**), Rājendra (**1033**), Kulottunga I (**1077**), name-for-name. Śrīvijaya (14, BOUNDED, Firm as observation): mature by **671** (>1,000 monks), a decade before its first inscription (682); Malayu absorbed (673, 689]. Śaṅkara (15, NO BITE, control): the silence is predicted on any date 600–900, and a datum predicted by all hypotheses discriminates among none.

## 5. Campaign 2: twenty-two graded cases

Campaign 2 aimed the method at targets at or beyond its reach — Kṛṣṇa, a legendary king, a rival religion that never used the channel — to map the edge. Of 22 graded cases: **2 CORRECTED, 4 CORROBORATED, 8 BOUNDED, 8 NO BITE** (D8 SKIPPED under its rule, excluded). The flagship is Vikramāditya.

### 5.1 Flagship: Vikramāditya, dissolved bearer by bearer

"Vikramāditya" ("Sun of Valour") is a title borne across five centuries, fused by legend into one immortal king of Ujjain who founded an era in 57 BCE, defeated the Śakas, and kept nine gems (the navaratnas, Kālidāsa among them). Graded bearer by bearer, the Chinese record pins **none by name** — and that assembled negative is the finding.

**C1, the era's founder (NO BITE).** The era is real; its name is the problem. Inscriptions of its first nine centuries call it Kṛta (343, 371), Kṛtā (404), or "the era of the Mālava people" (424) — never Vikrama. The Vikramāditya link appears in no source before the 9th century; the earliest tying inscription is dated **971 CE**, the earliest literary work Amitagati's *Subhāṣita-Ratna-Sandoha* (993–994). The Azes rescue is, in the records, discredited (Falk and Bennett: Azes epoch 47/46 BCE). Indian epigraphy dissolves the phantom; the Chinese silence is informed, since the Han stream covered the era's theatre (the Śaka–Yuezhi movements are its subject) yet names no king of Malwa or Ujjain. **C2, Candragupta II (BOUNDED, Probable).** The one fully historical bearer — inscriptions to Gupta year 93 = 412, title on his coinage — is the king Faxian lived under for a decade without naming. Case 12's frame corroboration stands; the naming claim fails for every bearer: the channel names kings only when a court event supplies a name. **C3, Skandagupta (BOUNDED, Probable).** Xuanzang's story names Bālāditya of Magadha defeating Mihirakula; the standard identification is Narasimhagupta Bālāditya, *not* Skandagupta (r. c. 455–467). The defeat tradition is corroborated under another king's name; the bearer is unreached. **C4, Yaśodharman (NO BITE).** His Mandasor inscriptions (Mālava year 589 = **532 CE**) claim Mihirakula's homage; no Chinese source names him, and the Chinese-carried victor tradition assigns the defeat to his rival, Bālāditya. **C5, the navaratna court (NO BITE; legend DISSOLVED on combined evidence).** The nine-gems legend rests on the pseudepigraphic *Jyotirvidābharaṇa*; the same complex made Bhartṛhari the king's brother — excluded by Case 7 (died c. 651–652). Kālidāsa's bracket is Indian-built: the Aihole praśasti (Śaka 556 = **634 CE**) names him a standard of poetic fame (firm TAQ; bracket 4th–5th century). No kāvya was translated into Chinese premodernly.

### 5.2 The Buddha and Mahāvīra bounds

**Buddha (A1–A3).** Both nirvāṇa intervals reached us through Chinese translation, each a lineage's cargo: the 218-year interval in the *Samantapāsādikā* (trans. Saṅghabhadra, **488** — who also placed the 975th dot in 489), the 100-year interval with the *Ayuwang zhuan* TAQ of **c. 306**. The translation dates date the disagreement, not the event (**A1: BOUNDED**, firm TAQs). China's own dates are constructions, excluded: the Zhao/Mu scheme (birth c. 1029–1027 BCE, death c. 949 BCE) and Fei Changfang's rival birth of **687 BCE** coexist in one catalogue tradition whose derivable death dates span approximately 350 years, built under the *mofa* countdown and the *huahu* polemic (**A2: CORRECTED, in reverse**). With Aśoka's consecration at c. 268 ± 5 BCE on the Greek synchronism of Rock Edict 13 (a firm, non-Chinese peg), the parinirvāṇa lies in **[c. 500, c. 350] BCE** on total evidence, and China narrows nothing inside it (**A3: NO BITE** on 486 vs c. 400 vs 368): China dates the carriers, not the carried. The Bechert consensus (c. 400 ± 30) stands on Indian-side grounds.

**Mahāvīra (E1, BOUNDED, Probable as a chain).** The traditional death of **527 BCE** is a medieval construction, shown from two sides: 470 years before the Vikrama era (57 + 470 = 527) and 605 years before the Śaka era (605 − 78 = 527) — two eras, two intervals, one answer, the Vikrama anchor dissolved in C1. Chained through the Pali predecease — the Buddhist canon reports Nātaputta's death at Pāvā in the Buddha's lifetime, an opponent's admission — the death falls in **[c. 500, c. 360] BCE**, late-5th-century centre (c. 430–400 on Bechert's). Who died first flips on the Jain synchronism: Hemacandra's 155 years before Candragupta (c. 321) gives c. 476 BCE. No Chinese source dates Mahāvīra; the bound is an Indian chain with every link labelled. Remaining E cases: the six-teachers list is bounded by the *Dīrghāgama* translation of **412–413 CE** (E2, BOUNDED, firm TAQ); Nirgranthas "the most numerous" at Puṇḍravardhana and Samataṭa c. 638 (E3, CORROBORATED, Probable); Pārśva (E4; 777 BCE = 527 + 250, pure chain arithmetic), Gosāla/Ājīvikas (E5), Ālāra/Uddaka (E6) are NO BITE.

### 5.3 Kṛṣṇa, the control; and the kings sweep

**Kṛṣṇa (B, NO BITE, Firm as a searched negative).** Its inventory is reported, not assumed. Kṛṣṇa is present in Buddhist literature that reached China — as past-life furniture in the *Ghaṭa Jātaka* (no. 454) and as a deva-name (*Heitian*) in assembly lists — but neither form carries a reign, era, or synchronism. The annals name no Kṛṣṇa, no war, no Pāṇḍavas. Xuanzang describes Mathurā at length without naming Kṛṣṇa and passes Sthāṇeśvara, on Kurukṣetra's edge, without a word of a war beside it; the *Mahābhārata* was never translated premodernly and appears in no Tang catalogue. The traditional war date (3139/3102 BCE) belongs to Kali-Yuga arithmetic; Zhang Qian's report (returned **126 BCE**) stands roughly three thousand years later. A method that cannot return an honest zero here could not be trusted returning a 31-year correction on Kashmir.

## 6. Discussion

**What the audit can do.** Where it bit, it bit in the same places: dated paper at a Chinese desk. A court record naming a king in a dated month (720; 1015, 1033, 1077) corroborates a bracket from outside; an annalistic sequence supplies events Indian evidence does not contain (647; 647–648); an investiture moves floruits by a generation (Kashmir); a colophon closes a hagiographic float (409); a physical instrument grades a magnitude impossible, not merely unlikely (Nalanda). The yield is narrow in kind — pegs, brackets, ceilings — and no corrected case rests on a pilgrim's reported interval. It also corrects in both directions, a condition of being a method at all: Xuanzang's "several hundred years" errs by roughly two centuries; China's own Buddha dates are excluded by the rule that excluded the dotted record; Yijing's "Śrīgupta, ~500 years ago" is tradition, not a foundation date.

**Limitations, stated explicitly.** (1) *Channel limits are structural*: no Brahmanical text of any date, no belles-lettres, no Jain embassy or bureau reached China — Śaṅkara, Kālidāsa, Kṛṣṇa, and Pārśva are unreachable in principle, and their silences map the channel, not the figures. (2) *Transmitted numbers are not independent numbers*; independence must be argued from custodianship, not geography. (3) *Load-bearing identifications are probable, not firm* — Bodiao = Vāsudeva I, Muduobi = Muktāpīḍa, Bālāditya = Narasimhagupta, Song Yun's king = Mihirakula, Aruṇāśa's identity — each verdict one grade below its annalistic events; if an identification fails, its bound fails with it. (4) *Silences are priced, not free*: the Dharmakīrti bracket rests partly on Xuanzang's silence (graded short of conclusive) and Faxian's corroboration is partly circular; both are held at Probable. (5) *The lag distribution is provisional* (median ~160–186 years, small sample). (6) *The Nalanda test measures an envelope, not a roster*, and Yijing's figure requires an estimated remainder; hence an interval peak, not a point. (7) *Corrections are local*: the Kashmir pegs do not repair Kalhaṇa's deep chronology. (8) *No textbook-misattribution survey exists in the underlying records.* (9) *Scope*: figures outside the preregistrations and the Nalanda dataset are not audited here.

**The set-level result.** Received figures fail in patterned ways: a disciple inflates a master by a measured factor of approximately 3; a title drifts a millennium and collects kings; a lineage's arithmetic returns from abroad dressed as independent corroboration; dates are manufactured on both sides of the Himalaya. The remedy is one operation — name the bearer, state the scope, grade the class, publish the no-bites with the corrections. The denominator is the finding: of 37 cases, 10 returned no bite and 13 only a bound. An audit reporting only its 7 corrections would commit the scope drift it exists to catch.
